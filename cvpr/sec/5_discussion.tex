\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The return from adding agents of one VLM is predictable from mean agent accuracy and $\phi$ (\cref{fig:teaser}). A MAS design can be tested by its effect on these quantities. The gain decomposition distinguishes improved agents from a better vote, as for Qwen (\cref{fig:panels}).

Shared errors reflect common perception and language priors when images do not decide the answer (\cref{fig:report,fig:prior}). Matched contrasts rank changes to perception and to the answering model above roles or contrast adjustment (\cref{fig:contrasts}). These contrasts predict that prompt diversity within one VLM cannot produce independent errors.

Debate trades disagreement for unanimity (\cref{fig:panels,fig:conformity}). Correct minority answers become wrong, especially on MedXpertQA-MM when they differ from the agent's blank-image answer. Keeping initial answers separates errors that communication corrects from those it creates.

An AI second reading in radiology is measured by its joint error with the first reading~\cite{geijer2018double}. Our contrasts predict greater complementarity from a different model than another instance of the same VLM (\cref{fig:contrasts}). Reader studies can test this prediction alongside the influence of wrong AI suggestions on radiologists~\cite{dratsch2023automation}.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The evaluation uses closed questions from public data sets and includes no human readers or clinical reporting.

\section{Conclusion}

Agents of one VLM share perception and the language prior, so they fail on the same items and majority vote and debate recover little. Error correlation, rather than the number of agents, sets what a MAS can gain. A second reading decorrelates errors when it comes from a different model, not a different prompt.
