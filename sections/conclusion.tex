\section{Conclusion}

We tested whether the scaling principles established for general-domain agent systems hold in
medicine, in the one regime those systems cannot reach: tasks with no tools. Three results follow.

The capability ceiling transfers, but as one edge of a two-sided window. Collaboration pays when a
single physician model scores roughly 25--50\% and not otherwise. This is a sharper and more
useful statement than a ceiling because it names both failure modes: on easy cases there is
nothing to gain, and on the hardest cases a panel of unreliable specialists reinforces its own
errors rather than correcting them. Splitting the data at the window separates it more than twice
as well as splitting at the ceiling ($t = 8.42$ against $t = 3.46$). The general-domain data are
consistent with a monotone ceiling only because their benchmarks never descend into the difficult
regime.

Coordination structure dominates panel size. Panel size does not predict collaboration gain at
all: a model containing it alone reaches cross-validated $R^2 = -0.006$, worse than predicting the
mean. Gains saturate at three agents. The largest improvements come from centralized coordination
in which an attending physician audits specialist opinions before committing. The
same architecture produces the largest loss in the study when the orchestrator is too weak to
audit ($-13.6$ pp), so the benefit is attributable to the audit, not to the topology. Where
coordination has no verification channel, error amplification --- the strongest term in our
scaling model --- goes uncontrolled and the panel discards correct answers it already held.
Coordination failure occurs at exactly $0.0\%$ under independent coordination, in this study as in
the general-domain one, because there is no channel on which it could occur.

The economics do not favour consultation outside the window. The architecture responsible for
every significant gain is also the least token-efficient by $4.3\times$, and outside the window a
budget-matched self-consistency control matches or beats every architecture we tested. For medical
deployments this converts an architectural question into a triage question: measure the
single-model baseline on the intended case mix first, and consult only where the window says
consultation will pay.
