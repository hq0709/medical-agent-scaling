\section{Introduction}

Ask six AI specialists a hard clinical question and let them confer. They will reach unanimity 98\%
of the time, be wrong half the times they agree, and --- by the moment they agree --- carry
$1.01$ independent opinions between them. Those three facts are one fact, and it is the subject of
this paper.

Multi-agent ``consultation'' has become the default design pattern in medical language-model
systems. MedAgents~\citep{tang2024medagents}, MDAgents~\citep{kim2024mdagents},
MAC~\citep{chen2025mac} and TeamMedAgents~\citep{teammedagents} all instantiate the same clinical
intuition --- a panel of specialists should diagnose better than one generalist, because a tumour
board diagnoses better than one physician --- and the pattern is being deployed faster than it is
being measured.

The most complete measurement to date is not in medicine. \citet{kim2026scaling} evaluated 180
controlled configurations across five canonical architectures, three model families and six
agentic benchmarks under matched token budgets, and derived a quantitative scaling principle for
agent systems. Three findings bear directly on clinical deployment. A \emph{capability ceiling}
($\beta = -0.408$, $p<0.001$): once a single agent exceeds roughly 45\% accuracy, additional
agents yield negative returns. A \emph{tool--coordination trade-off} ($\beta = -0.330$,
$p<0.001$): tool-heavy tasks pay an overhead that erodes any collaborative gain. And
\emph{architecture-dependent error amplification}: independent panels amplify individual errors
$17.2\times$ relative to a single agent, while centralized coordination contains them to
$4.4\times$ through a validation bottleneck at the orchestrator.

Medicine was excluded from that study, and the exclusion is more interesting than it looks. The
45\% threshold sits exactly where expert-level medical question answering lives: frontier models
score 38--56\% on MedXpertQA~\citep{zuo2025medxpertqa} while exceeding 90\% on
MedQA~\citep{jin2021medqa}, so a single medical benchmark suite straddles the threshold from both
sides. And the tool--coordination trade-off, the strongest confound in the general-domain result,
is absent by construction from medical multiple-choice reasoning: the task uses \emph{no tools at
all}. Medicine is therefore not merely an untested domain but the $T = 0$ limiting case of the
scaling principle --- the one slice of the design space where coordination can be measured with
the tool-overhead term removed, and the one regime hard enough to reveal what happens below the
threshold rather than above it.

We run that experiment at matched scale: 180 multi-agent configurations, 63{,}499 episodes, three
capability tiers, three medical benchmarks, five architectures, panel sizes $N \in
\{1,3,5,7,9\}$, and a budget-matched single-model control at every cell.

Our central finding is that consultation in medicine violates the arithmetic premise it inherits
from human practice. Pooling judgements helps physicians because physicians fail
differently~\citep{kurvers2016boosting, kammer2017}. Language-model agents prompted into different
specialties do not: their pairwise error correlation is $\varphi = 0.73$, so a nine-member panel
carries $1.31$ effective independent opinions, and peer discussion raises the correlation to
$0.986$. The panel does not converge on an answer; it collapses onto one.

Three observable consequences follow, and they are the same fact seen three ways. Panel size
predicts nothing, because tripling the panel adds a tenth of an opinion. Consensus carries no
information, because it is agreement among copies --- unanimity reaches 98\% while accuracy given
unanimity stays at chance, and confidence rises most on the answers that are wrong. And the
benefit that survives is confined to a narrow \emph{window} of task difficulty rather than bounded
by a ceiling: the general-domain benchmarks never descend far enough to see the window's lower
edge, which is why they report a ceiling where the medical data show a band.
