\section{Introduction}

Ask six AI specialists a hard clinical question and let them confer. They will reach unanimity 98\%
of the time. They will be wrong half the times they agree. And by the moment they agree, they
carry $1.01$ independent opinions between them. Those three facts are one fact, and it is the
subject of this paper.

Multi-agent ``consultation'' has become the default design pattern in medical language-model
systems. MedAgents~\citep{tang2024medagents}, MDAgents~\citep{kim2024mdagents},
MAC~\citep{chen2025mac} and TeamMedAgents~\citep{teammedagents} all instantiate the same clinical
intuition. A panel of specialists should diagnose better than one generalist, the reasoning goes,
because a tumour board diagnoses better than one physician. The pattern is being deployed faster
than it is being measured.

The most complete measurement to date is not in medicine. \citet{kim2026scaling} evaluated 180
controlled configurations across five canonical architectures, three model families and six
agentic benchmarks under matched token budgets, and derived a quantitative scaling principle for
agent systems. Three findings bear directly on clinical deployment. A \emph{capability ceiling}
($\beta = -0.408$, $p<0.001$): once a single agent exceeds roughly 45\% accuracy, additional
agents yield negative returns. A \emph{tool--coordination trade-off} ($\beta = -0.330$,
$p<0.001$): tool-heavy tasks pay an overhead that erodes any collaborative gain. And
\emph{architecture-dependent error amplification}: independent panels amplify individual errors
$17.2\times$ relative to a single agent, while centralized coordination contains them to
$4.4\times$ through a validation bottleneck at the orchestrator.

Medicine was excluded from that study, and the exclusion is more interesting than it looks. The
45\% threshold sits exactly where expert-level medical question answering lives. Frontier models
score 38--56\% on MedXpertQA~\citep{zuo2025medxpertqa} while exceeding 90\% on
MedQA~\citep{jin2021medqa}, so a single medical benchmark suite straddles the threshold from both
sides. And the tool--coordination trade-off, the strongest confound in the general-domain result,
is absent by construction from medical multiple-choice reasoning, because the task uses
\emph{no tools at all}. Medicine is therefore the $T = 0$ limiting case of the scaling principle. It is the one slice of
the design space where coordination can be measured with the tool-overhead term removed, and the
one regime hard enough to reveal what happens below the threshold rather than above it.

We run 180 multi-agent configurations and 63{,}499 episodes across three capability tiers, three
medical benchmarks, five architectures, panel sizes $N \in \{1,3,5,7,9\}$, and a budget-matched
single-model control at every cell. Two measurements are new here. The error correlation between
panel members, which turns out to govern everything else, is not reported in prior agent-scaling
work; nor is the effect of coordination on the calibration of the panel's own confidence.

Our central finding is that consultation in medicine violates the arithmetic premise it inherits
from human practice. Pooling judgements helps physicians because physicians fail
differently~\citep{kurvers2016boosting, kammer2017}. Language-model agents prompted into different
specialties do not fail differently. Their pairwise error correlation is $\varphi = 0.73$, which
leaves a nine-member panel with $1.31$ effective independent opinions, and peer discussion raises
the correlation to $0.986$. The panel does not converge on an answer. It collapses onto one.

Three observable consequences follow. Panel size predicts nothing, because tripling the panel adds
a tenth of an opinion. Consensus carries no information, because it is agreement among copies:
unanimity reaches 98\% while accuracy given unanimity stays at chance, and confidence rises most
on the answers that are wrong. The benefit that survives is confined to a narrow \emph{window} of
task difficulty rather than bounded by a ceiling. General-domain benchmarks never descend far
enough to see the window's lower edge, which is why a ceiling is reported there where the medical
data show a band.
