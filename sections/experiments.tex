\section{Experiments and results}

\subsection{Setup}

\paragraph{LLMs and capability scaling.}

Three OpenAI models form capability tiers T1--T3 for the architecture grid
(Table~\ref{tab:capability}; the screening probe over eight candidates is
Table~\ref{tab:tiers}). The tiers are monotone on all three benchmarks and span
single-doctor accuracy from 15.6\% to 91.6\% (15.6\% to 41.6\% on MedXpertQA), straddling the
45\% threshold reported by \citet{kim2026scaling}. Five further models (Gemini~3.5~Flash~Lite and Gemini~3.7~Flash from
Google, Claude~Haiku~4.5 and Claude~Sonnet~5 from Anthropic, and
\texttt{deepseek-v4-flash} from DeepSeek) run on the same items and architecture grid.
Five more (\texttt{deepseek-v4-pro}, \texttt{qwen3.8-flash} and \texttt{qwen3.8-max},
\texttt{glm-5.3-flash} and \texttt{glm-5.3}) contribute single-doctor baselines on the same
items for the diversity analysis of \S\ref{sec:premise}. Thirteen models across six vendors span
$I = 34.0$ to $71.1$. Capability-matched cross-vendor pairs include
\texttt{deepseek-v4-pro} at $59.7$ with \texttt{gpt-5-mini} at $59.2$, and
Gemini~3.5~Flash~Lite at $50.3$ with \texttt{gpt-5-nano} at $50.8$.
A task-grounded capability metric explains more variance than a generic
intelligence index~\citep{kim2026scaling}, so we define the capability index $I$ as each model's
mean single-doctor chain-of-thought accuracy across the three benchmarks. A cell denotes one
model--benchmark pair.




\paragraph{Budget-matched controls.}

Every configuration is accompanied by three single-model controls: zero-shot,
chain-of-thought, and self-consistency at a matched budget. The self-consistency pool is built from cached
$n{=}8$ sampling calls (the API maximum), so any $k$ is a prefix of the same samples. Nominal cost
is charged as $\lceil k/8 \rceil$ prompts plus $k$ completions, which is what an efficient
implementation would pay. Panels are compared against the self-consistency configuration whose
mean per-item \emph{dollar} cost is closest, not the one with a matching sample count. Matching on
samples silently favours the panel, because a panel pays the prompt once per agent while
self-consistency amortises it across a batch.

Cost accounting is \emph{nominal} throughout. Cached calls are charged at list price, so the
economics figures report what a configuration would cost to run, not what our cache made it cost.

\paragraph{Statistics.}

Accuracies are means over a benchmark's $250$ items, and summaries across cells or configurations
report the mean and standard deviation.

Episodes that fail for infrastructure reasons (rate limits, timeouts) are
recorded with an error status and excluded from accuracy. Scoring them as wrong answers would
bias call-heavy architectures downward.

\paragraph{Architectures differ little within most cells.}
At their best panel sizes, the four architectures differ little within a cell, except on
\texttt{deepseek-v4-flash}, where Hybrid trails the other three architectures by $4.4$--$8.8$\,pp
on all three benchmarks. The ordering of topologies (star, complete, tiered, or no edges) carries
only weakly across benchmarks. Kendall's $\tau$ between the architecture rankings of any two
benchmarks averages $+0.333$, and leave-one-benchmark-out cross-validation gives $R^2 = -0.047$.
On \texttt{gpt-5-nano} / MedXpertQA, Independent peaks at 35.6\% at $N{=}5$, while Decentralized
reaches 38.0\% at $N{=}3$. Gains mostly arrive by $N{=}3$. A panel larger than $N{=}5$ helps
in $29$ of $96$ (cell, architecture) pairs, by at most $2.8$\,pp (Fig.~\ref{fig:nscaling},
Fig.~\ref{fig:dist}).


\paragraph{Both factors are small, and they move apart.}
Eq.~\ref{eq:decomp} separates headroom from the capture rate $\kappa$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:decomp}).
Above $\PSA = 25\%$, the two factors move in opposite directions, an empirical result that the
factorisation does not require (Table~\ref{tab:ceiling}). Headroom falls from $+14.3$\,pp at
$\PSA \in [25,50)$ to $+4.1$ above $70\%$, while $\kappa$ rises from $+6.6\%$ to $+29.5\%$.
Gain stays between $-0.3$ and $+1.2$\,pp across the four bands.

\subsection{Is anyone in the room right? The arithmetic premise}
\label{sec:premise}

The headroom in Eq.~\ref{eq:decomp} measures how much independent judgement a panel contains.

\paragraph{Sampling supplies most of the headroom.}
At $n_a{=}9$ the single doctor scores $53.1\%$ and the oracle ceiling $63.9\%$: the panel
has $10.7$\,pp of headroom.

\begin{figure*}[t]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig10_ceiling.pdf}

(b) Capture rate $\kappa$ is pooled over configurations at $N\geq3$: total gain divided by total headroom. Zero marks the single-doctor baseline; $100$ marks an oracle selecting a correct answer whenever a member holds one.}
\label{fig:ceiling}
\end{figure*}

Self-consistency gives one model the same generic
prompt nine times at one temperature; a panel gives it nine different specialty prompts. Both
produce nine answers, so both admit the same oracle measurement. Nine self-consistency samples
put a correct answer on the table $63.5\%$ of the time; nine distinct specialists put one there
$63.9\%$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:ceiling}a). Repeated sampling supplies $10.4$\,pp ($97\%$) of the
$10.7$\,pp of headroom, and specialty role prompts contribute $+0.37$\,pp.

\paragraph{A nine-member panel contains 1.27 independent opinions.}
Across Independent, Centralized and Decentralized at $n_a \geq 3$, the mean pairwise error
correlation is $\varphi = 0.758$. These architectures reuse the same first opinions
(\S\ref{sec:nested}), so their first-round correlations coincide by design. Hybrid is reported
separately because its $\varphi$ is measured only on escalated items (\S\ref{sec:ceiling}).
By Eq.~\ref{eq:neff} this leaves $N_{\mathrm{eff}} = 1.19$ at $N{=}3$, $1.24$
at $N{=}5$ and $1.27$ at $N{=}9$ (Table~\ref{tab:neff}). Tripling the panel adds
$0.08$ of an independent opinion. Panel size alone predicts gain at cross-validated
$R^2 = -0.007$ over the $225$ OpenAI configurations, worse than predicting the mean
(\S\ref{app:scaling}).

\paragraph{Discussion drives the effective panel size to one.}
Across the $96$ Decentralized configurations, peer discussion raises $\varphi$ from $0.758$
(SD $0.117$) before the exchange to $0.990$ (SD $0.010$) after it (Fig.~\ref{fig:slope}).
At $\varphi = 0.990$ a nine-agent panel has $N_{\text{eff}} = 1.01$.

\paragraph{What discussion changes.}
Across $42{,}464$ (item, panel position) pairs,
$37.4\%$ of opinions change between the first round and the last. Of the changes, $39.0\%$ go from wrong to right,
$21.4\%$ from right to wrong, and $39.6\%$ from one wrong answer to a different wrong
one. Three-fifths of all movement ends somewhere other than the correct option. The net effect on
individual opinions is positive ($2{,}787$ more corrections than corruptions, or $+6.6$\,pp of
positions), while the members' error correlation rises.

\paragraph{Role prompting matters most without communication.}
We measure how much accuracy gain specialty role prompts contribute by comparing specialist
panels with the \textsc{Generic panel} condition. Arms
share the model, the temperature, the architectures and the same $250$ items
(\texttt{gpt-5-nano}, MedXpertQA), and we pair them over the $12$ matched
(architecture, $N \geq 3$) configurations. Against the single doctor at $32.0\%$, the specialist
panel gains $+4.63$\,pp. The Generic panel supplies $+2.77$\,pp (SD $2.0$ across the $12$
configurations), and specialty role prompts supply $+1.87$\,pp (SD $1.4$).
The role prompts' share of the gain is $81\%$ under independent voting, $40\%$ under a central
orchestrator and $22\%$ under discussion. Independent generic internist panels gain $+0.50$\,pp
averaged over $N = 3$--$9$.

\paragraph{Specialty matching has a negative mean effect across models.}
Every MedXpertQA item is labelled with the body system it concerns, and
the router ranks specialties by relevance, so at a given panel size some items receive a panel
containing the corresponding specialty (a neurologist on a nervous-system case) and others do
not. We compare these groups to measure the effect of specialty matching.

We compare the \emph{gain over the single doctor} on the same items, which absorbs item difficulty
into the baseline. Across eight models at $n_a \in \{3,5\}$, matching the specialist to the case
changes gain by $-1.27$\,pp on average across $16$ contrasts, ranging from $-12.5$ to $+11.9$\,pp.

\paragraph{Normalising correlation reduces the accuracy-gap coefficient.}
Training families are generations within one vendor, such as \texttt{gpt-4o}, \texttt{gpt-4.1}
and \texttt{gpt-5}. We regress the $360$ model--benchmark pairs in Table~\ref{tab:diversity} on
family identity and accuracy gap, using the normalisation defined in \S\ref{sec:metrics}.
On raw $\varphi$ the accuracy-gap coefficient is $\hat\beta = -0.725$ ($R^2 = 0.328$).
On $\varphi/\varphi_{\max}$ it is $-0.034$ ($R^2 = 0.015$), while the family term remains
positive ($+0.107 \to +0.088$).

\paragraph{Mixing training families reduces correlation more than mixing vendors.}
Table~\ref{tab:diversity} compares the four sources of panel diversity.
Specialty prompts give $\varphi/\varphi_{\max} = 0.792$. Different checkpoints from one family
give $0.561$, and different training families within one vendor give $0.465$.
The latter yield $2.60$ effective opinions in a nine-member panel, computed from raw $\varphi$.
The $300$ cross-vendor model--benchmark pairs over the same $750$ items span six vendors on two
continents (OpenAI, Google, Anthropic, DeepSeek, Alibaba and Zhipu).
They give $\varphi/\varphi_{\max} = 0.504$ and $N_{\text{eff}} = 2.29$ on raw $\varphi$.
The same-vendor coefficient on normalised correlation is $\hat\beta_{\text{same vendor}} = -0.032$,
with the family term at $+0.088$. Restricted to pairs whose error rates differ by less than
$5$\,pp, where $\varphi_{\max}$ averages $0.9$, cross-vendor pairs give $0.480$ against
$0.386$ within a vendor ($2.02$ against $2.31$ effective opinions on raw $\varphi$).



\paragraph{Accuracy gaps correlate with raw error correlation.}
Across the $360$ model--benchmark pairs, the correlation between accuracy gap and raw error
correlation is $r = -0.550$. Pairs within $5$\,pp of each other have mean
$\varphi = 0.438$, giving $2.00$ effective opinions for nine members by Eq.~\ref{eq:neff}.
Pairs more than $30$\,pp apart have mean $\varphi = 0.156$, giving $4.00$ effective opinions.

After normalisation by Eq.~\ref{eq:phimax}, the correlation with accuracy gap is $r = -0.043$.

A panel of one strong, one mid and one weak specialist has lower accuracy than three strong
members under both Centralized and Decentralized on MedXpertQA (\S\ref{app:hetero}).

\paragraph{Most headroom requires choosing a member per item.}
Two oracles separate fixed specialty selection from per-item selection. Always trusting whichever
specialty position turns out best over the whole benchmark, a post-hoc choice no deployment could
make in advance, reaches $54.6\%$ against $53.1\%$ for the single doctor.
Choosing a correct opinion item by item reaches $63.9\%$. Of the $10.7$\,pp of headroom, $1.5$
comes from picking a better specialist and $9.3$ requires changing which member is selected
from case to case.

\subsection{Can the room tell who? The capture rate}
\label{sec:ceiling}

The capture rate $\kappa$, averaged over all configurations at $n_a \geq 3$, is $17.7\%$ for
peer discussion, $14.8\%$ for an auditing orchestrator, $8.6\%$ for budget-matched
self-consistency and $4.9\%$ for majority vote. Hybrid has $\kappa = -7.4\%$, below the
single-doctor baseline
(Fig.~\ref{fig:ceiling}b). Four-fifths of the headroom is discarded by the best mechanism we test,
and choosing the best architecture for each cell after the fact, which no deployment can do,
reaches $\kappa = 23.3\%$ using the same estimator as the per-architecture values.
At $n_a{=}9$ the best measured architecture scores $54.9\%$.

\paragraph{Disagreement includes pairs of wrong answers.}
Two specialists in the same panel give different answers on $14.1\%$ of items before any exchange
(MedXpertQA $19.6\%$, MedAgentsBench $17.1\%$, MedQA $5.6\%$). The corresponding rate is $21\%$ for
second opinions that produce a distinctly different diagnosis in a large second-opinion audit
\citep{vansuch2017}. Across
all pairs, $48.7\%$ agree and are right, $37.2\%$ agree and are both wrong,
$8.4\%$ split with exactly one right, and $5.7\%$ split with both wrong. Of every disagreement,
$40.2\%$ is two agents reaching different wrong answers.

\paragraph{Error amplification locates the loss inside the episode.}
Error amplification $\Ae$ is $0.32$ for Centralized, $0.29$ for Independent,
$0.25$ for Decentralized and $0.09$ for Hybrid.

Mean rationale overlap among first opinions is
$0.383$ for pairs that are both right, $0.346$ for pairs that are both wrong and $0.199$ for pairs
with one right and one wrong answer. Overlap differs by $0.15$--$0.18$ between pairs with the
same correctness and pairs with mixed correctness, and by $0.037$ between both-right and
both-wrong pairs. Similar rationales occur when both members are right and when both are wrong.



\paragraph{First-round agreement signals weakly distinguish correct answers.}
Across $71{,}793$ panel episodes at $N \geq 3$, first-round answer entropy predicts panel
correctness at AUC $0.592$ and Youden $J = 0.177$. Majority share reaches AUC $0.591$ and
$J = 0.177$. Members' errors correlate at $\varphi = 0.76$, and panels agree both on correct
answers and on shared errors.

\paragraph{A selector trained on the answers does not beat counting votes.}
For each of the $71{,}793$ panel
episodes we formed one row per distinct candidate answer, with fifteen features computable from
the panel's own first round: vote share, margin over the runner-up, the mean, maximum, minimum and
spread of the confidences attached to that candidate, that candidate's confidence relative to the
panel mean, rationale lengths, the number of distinct answers, the entropy of the vote
distribution, and panel size. The label is whether the candidate is correct. We trained a logistic
regression and a gradient-boosting classifier under five-fold cross-validation grouped by item:
fitted on ground truth and tested in the same distribution using these fifteen features.

Neither beats majority vote. Selecting the highest-scoring candidate gives $53.55\%$ (logistic)
and $53.37\%$ (boosted trees) against $53.65\%$ for taking the plurality, while the oracle reaches
$61.95\%$. Rescaled so that plurality scores $0$ and the oracle $100$, the two learned selectors
score $-1.3$ and $-3.4$. Neither classifier recovers any of the $8.30$\,pp between plurality and
oracle using these fifteen features.

\paragraph{Hybrid escalates on one-fifth of items.}
Hybrid escalates on $20\%$ of items overall. The rate varies across model--benchmark cells:
$76.8\%$ for \texttt{claude-sonnet-5} on
MedXpertQA, $0.0\%$ for \texttt{gpt-4.1-nano} on MedQA, where the gate never once fires in
$1{,}000$ episodes (its $250$ items at four panel sizes).

\subsection{The same measurement on two published frameworks}
\label{sec:frameworks}

To measure systems we did not design, we ran
MedAgents~\citep{tang2024medagents} and MDAgents~\citep{kim2024mdagents} on the same
$750$ items, from the prompts in their own repositories rather than from the papers'
descriptions (\S\ref{app:frameworks}).

The frameworks differ in routing, discussion and aggregation. MedAgents
partitions each question into five clinical domains and two option domains on the
fly, has each domain expert write an analysis, synthesises them into one report, and
then puts the report to a vote, revising it until no expert objects. MDAgents first
classifies the question as basic, intermediate or advanced, answers the basic ones
with a single agent, and for the rest recruits a panel and lets its members choose
whom to talk to, round by round, until nobody wants to speak. Peer context also differs:
a MedAgents voter reads a report of roughly $8{,}000$ characters, whereas our members share $900$.

\paragraph{The frameworks have similar error correlations.}
MedAgents gains $+5.7$\,pp over the single doctor and MDAgents $+1.2$
(Table~\ref{tab:frameworks}). Their error correlations are $0.860$ and $0.863$.
Across all six framework--benchmark combinations $\varphi$ stays between $0.803$ and
$0.890$, on baselines running from $15.6\%$ to $66.0\%$, and both frameworks are
more correlated than our \texttt{gpt-4.1-nano} panels at $\varphi = 0.792$.
Six experts amount to $1.1$ independent opinions; nine members of our
\texttt{gpt-4.1-nano} panels amount to $1.23$.

\paragraph{Revision ends early; complexity tracks difficulty on one benchmark.}
MedAgents revises its report until no expert votes against it. That loop reaches
agreement on $96$--$99\%$ of items and does so \emph{in the first round} on
$92$--$96\%$. Its revision loop stops when no expert objects, with no check on correctness.
MDAgents routes on a complexity label that tracks difficulty only on MedXpertQA, where the
items it calls most complex score $14\%$ against $18$ and $21$.
On MedAgentsBench its three levels differ by at most five points, while on MedQA the basic tier
scores lowest ($59$ against $74$ and $70$).

\subsection{What a panel costs}

\paragraph{Centralized is the least token-efficient architecture.}
Successes per 1{,}000 tokens: Hybrid $0.75$, Independent $0.39$, Decentralized $0.34$,
self-consistency $0.25$, Centralized $0.20$. Centralized is $3.7\times$ less token-efficient than
Hybrid, the most efficient condition.


On MedXpertQA at \texttt{gpt-5-nano}, Decentralized at $N{=}3$ reaches 38.0\% while
self-consistency at the closest matched dollar cost reaches 34.8\%. On MedQA at the same tier,
self-consistency at $k{=}15$ reaches 88.4\% and costs three times as much as the best panel,
which reaches 89.6\%. On MedAgentsBench at T1, self-consistency (20.0\%) trails Centralized (21.6\%).

\paragraph{What a point of accuracy costs.}
Table~\ref{tab:ppd} reports accuracy gain per dollar of additional spend
for each cell's best configuration. Among configurations costing more than the single doctor,
the cost multiple ranges from $1.9\times$ to $11.5\times$, and gain per additional dollar varies
by two orders of magnitude. It is
$7.6$ and $4.3$ percentage points per additional dollar per thousand questions on the two
MedXpertQA cells with the largest gains, against $0.34$ on MedAgentsBench at T3, where a
$+2.4$\,pp gain costs $11.5\times$. On MedQA at T3 the best configuration is Hybrid at $n_a{=}1$,
which costs less than the single-doctor baseline.
