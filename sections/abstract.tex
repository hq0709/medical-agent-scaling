Multi-agent ``consultation'' has become the default design pattern in medical language-model
systems, on the intuition that a panel of specialists diagnoses better than one physician. That
intuition inherits an arithmetic premise from human medicine: pooling helps because the opinions
pooled are independent. We find the premise does not hold. Across 180 multi-agent
configurations and 63{,}499 episodes, spanning three capability tiers, three medical benchmarks,
five canonical architectures and panel sizes $N \in \{1,3,5,7,9\}$, the mean pairwise error
correlation between panel members is $\varphi = 0.73$. A nine-member panel therefore carries
$1.31$ effective independent opinions, and peer discussion raises $\varphi$ to $0.986$, leaving a
six-agent consultation informationally equivalent to $1.01$ physicians at the moment it reaches
consensus.

Three consequences follow, and they are the same fact seen three ways. Panel size carries no
predictive value whatsoever (cross-validated $R^2 = -0.006$ alone), because tripling the panel
adds one tenth of an independent opinion. Discussion raises unanimity from 66\% to 98\% while the
error rate given unanimity stays at 50\%, and raises confidence on wrong answers more than on
right ones, compressing the gap that separates them by 45\%. Agreement among copies looks like corroboration. The benefit that does exist is confined to a \emph{window} of task
difficulty rather than bounded by a ceiling. Configurations whose single-doctor baseline falls
between 25\% and 50\% gain $+2.48$ pp while those outside lose $0.31$ pp (Welch $t = 8.42$,
$p < 10^{-6}$), with all 20 significant improvements inside the window and all 10 significant
degradations outside it.

The medical setting is what makes these quantities measurable. Multiple-choice clinical reasoning
uses no tools, so the tool--coordination trade-off that dominates the
general-domain law vanishes identically, and expert-level items drive single-agent accuracy to
15\%, far below any existing agentic benchmark. The reported 45\% capability ceiling
reproduces as the window's upper edge. Its lower edge is visible only at medical difficulty. What
does not reproduce is the value of coordination itself. An attending-physician architecture that
audits before aggregating yields the study's largest gain ($+7.6$ pp, $p = 2\times10^{-5}$) and,
when the auditor is too weak, its largest loss ($-13.6$ pp, $p = 10^{-5}$).
