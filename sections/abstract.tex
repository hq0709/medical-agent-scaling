Multi-agent ``consultation'' has become the default design pattern in medical language-model
systems, on the intuition that a panel of specialists diagnoses better than one physician. We test
that intuition at the scale at which it has been tested for general-domain agents, and in the one
regime those studies cannot reach. Medical multiple-choice reasoning uses no tools, so the
tool--coordination trade-off that dominates the general-domain scaling law vanishes identically,
isolating coordination itself; and expert-level medical items drive single-agent accuracy down to
15\%, far below anything in existing agentic benchmarks. Across 180 multi-agent configurations and
63{,}499 episodes --- three capability tiers, three medical benchmarks, five canonical
architectures, panel sizes $N \in \{1,3,5,7,9\}$, budget-matched single-model controls throughout
--- three results follow.

Collaboration is bounded by a \emph{window} of task difficulty, not a ceiling. Configurations
whose single-doctor baseline falls between 25\% and 50\% gain $+2.48$ pp; those outside lose
$0.31$ pp (Welch $t = 8.42$, $p < 10^{-6}$). The separation is two-sided and without overlap: all
20 configurations that significantly beat the single doctor lie inside the window, all 10 that
significantly lose to it lie outside. The 45\% capability ceiling reported for general-domain
agents is reproduced as the window's upper edge; its lower edge is visible only at medical
difficulty.

Panel size is not the design variable. It carries no predictive value whatsoever
(cross-validated $R^2 = -0.006$ alone), gains saturate at three agents, and what distinguishes the
best architecture from the worst is whether a competent agent audits the panel before it commits.
Specialist capability matters $4.1\times$ more than the orchestrator's, and an orchestrator too
weak to audit produces the largest degradation in the study ($-13.6$ pp, $p = 10^{-5}$) by
overriding a correct majority.

Consultation degrades the signal a clinician would use to catch its errors. Peer discussion raises
unanimity from 66\% to 98\% while leaving the error rate given unanimity at 50\%; it raises
confidence on wrong answers more than on right ones, compressing the confidence gap that separates
them by 45\%. Accuracy gains of $6$--$8$ pp inside the window are real, but the panel output a
clinician would most readily act on --- unanimous and confident --- has been made the least
diagnostic of its own reliability.
