Multi-agent consultation is the default design pattern in medical language-model systems.
Several agents are prompted into clinical specialties and deliberate until they agree, on the
reasoning that makes a second opinion worth soliciting: when specialists who make different
mistakes converge on a diagnosis, the convergence is itself evidence. Although that reasoning
requires the specialists to make different mistakes, no evaluation of a medical agent panel has
measured whether its members do. In this work, we measure it, across $480$ multi-agent
configurations and $161{,}999$ clinical episodes. They do not: a nine-member panel speaks with
one voice on $66\%$ of items and is wrong on $38\%$ of what it agrees on. Their errors correlate at
$\varphi = 0.76$, so nine agents carry $1.27$ independent opinions and, once they have
deliberated, $1.01$. That number does not move. It does not move when the specialty roster
changes, when the panel grows, when the vendor boundary is crossed to six independently trained
ecosystems on two continents, or when two published medical agent frameworks are run from their
own code: their gains differ by a factor of five and their error correlations are $0.860$ and
$0.863$. In simpler terms: a panel of nine is worth one, and it agrees anyway---so its agreement
must not reach a clinician as confidence.
